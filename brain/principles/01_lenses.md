# Lenses — how to read a story, by kind

There is no house worldview. Each story is read through the lens that fits it,
and a story that fits none of these is still a story. The question is always
the same: **what happened, why, who and what it touches — and what it means
for the reader's money.** The reader holds Indian equities (via Zerodha),
Australian equities and gold (via Webull AU) and some US stock, and lives in
Australia. Their markets are India, Australia and the US, in that order of
exposure; Europe, China and Japan matter through what they transmit.

## Central banks
What was decided, the vote, and the change in guidance — against what the
market had priced. The gap between the two is the story; a decision fully
priced is not news. Follow it into the currency, the front end of the curve,
bank stocks and rate-sensitive sectors (property, utilities, long-duration
growth). RBI and RBA decisions are first-class, not footnotes to the Fed.

## Fiscal and government
Budgets, deficits, tariffs, taxes, subsidies, bond issuance, ratings. Ask who
pays and who gains: which sectors get money, which lose a protection, how the
financing lands on the bond market. A policy announcement is not a policy;
say what has been legislated versus floated.

## Earnings and companies
Results against expectations, and above all **guidance** — what management says
about the next year. A beat with cut guidance is a cut. Read through to peers
and suppliers: one company's demand comment is often a sector's news. For a
name the reader holds, state plainly whether the news strengthens or weakens
the case for holding it.

## Markets and flows
What moved, how far against its normal range, and on what. Breadth (how many
names joined in), sector leadership, foreign and domestic flows (FPI and DII
in India), and positioning. A move without news is a finding too — say it is
unexplained rather than inventing a cause.

## Currencies
Rates differentials, trade and capital flows, intervention. INR and AUD are the
reader's own: a weaker rupee raises an Indian importer's costs and an IT
exporter's revenue; a weaker Australian dollar flatters miners' AUD earnings
and raises the cost of US holdings. Always say what a currency move does to
the reader's positions in their home currency.

## Commodities and energy
Supply (OPEC+, outages, mine supply, weather), demand (China, the industrial
cycle), inventories and the futures curve. Then transmission: oil is an
Indian inflation and current-account story (India imports most of its crude),
iron ore and copper are an Australian earnings and AUD story, gold is a
reserve and real-rate story among others — not the centre of the world.

## Credit and banks
Spreads, defaults, bank funding, deposit flows and asset quality. Stress in
credit usually reaches equities second; say if it is spreading or contained.

## Geopolitics
Name the market channel or leave it out: shipping lanes, energy supply,
sanctions on a sector, tariffs on a product, an election that changes fiscal
direction. Conflict without a transmission path is not a market story.

## India
RBI and the rupee; inflation and the monsoon; government capex and the fiscal
path; credit growth and bank asset quality; IT services against US demand and
the dollar; oil import costs; FPI versus domestic SIP flows; regulation (SEBI)
that changes market structure. Mid- and small-caps behave differently from the
NIFTY — say which the story concerns.

## Australia
RBA and housing credit; the miners against China demand and iron ore; the
banks against rates and mortgage arrears; household spending and the big
retailers; superannuation flows; the AUD as the transmission for almost all
of it.

## How lenses combine
Most real stories cross two or three lenses — a tariff is fiscal, a currency
story and an earnings story at once. Follow the chain until it reaches the
reader's markets or stops mattering, and stop there. Do not force a story
into gold, the dollar or sovereign debt because those were once the default;
include them when the story genuinely runs through them.
