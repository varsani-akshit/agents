# Debasement, Financial Repression, and Real Rates

## Real rates are the master variable for gold
Nominal yield minus expected inflation. Gold pays no coupon, so its opportunity
cost is the real yield available on a risk-free alternative. When real rates fall
(especially below zero), the opportunity cost of holding gold vanishes and gold
tends to rise. When real rates rise, gold faces a headwind.

Watch `DFII10` (10y TIPS real yield) and `T10YIE` (10y breakeven). The
relationship is strong but not mechanical — when it breaks down, that breakdown is
itself the signal, and usually means something else has become dominant: a
confidence shock, official-sector buying, or a liquidity event forcing
indiscriminate selling.

## Financial repression: the quiet lever
Holding nominal rates below the rate of inflation transfers wealth from savers
(bondholders) to debtors (usually the government). It is the least visible of
Dalio's four levers, and it works by stealth over years rather than in a single
event. Its instruments:
- Keeping policy rates below inflation
- Buying duration (QE) or capping yields (YCC) to suppress the long end
- Regulatory pressure on banks, pensions, and insurers to hold government paper
- Treasury debt-management choices: shortening maturity, buybacks, bill-heavy
  issuance to avoid testing long-end demand
- Tolerating inflation overshoots while describing them as temporary

**Repression is deliberately hard to see.** It shows up not in announcements but
in the *composition* of issuance, the *duration* of the debt stock, and the gap
between where yields are and where they would clear without official support.

## The debasement trade
When markets price a higher probability of the monetisation lever, capital moves
toward assets whose supply cannot be expanded by decree: gold, silver, bitcoin,
and to a lesser extent equities in real-asset-heavy sectors. The signature is
**several of these rising together while the currency weakens** — correlation
across otherwise unrelated assets is the tell that the common factor is the
currency itself, not anything specific to each asset.

A useful discipline: when gold, BTC, and foreign currencies all rise against the
dollar simultaneously, the story is probably about the dollar. When gold rises
and BTC falls, the story is probably about risk aversion.

## Gold/silver ratio
Gold/silver measures the market's relative appetite for monetary versus
industrial exposure to the metals complex.
- **Ratio rising (silver lagging):** monetary demand dominating, or industrial
  demand weakening, or risk aversion — silver is the higher-beta metal and lags
  in a fear-driven move.
- **Ratio falling (silver leading):** typically later in a metals bull market
  when speculative participation broadens, or when industrial demand is strong.

Historically the ratio is mean-reverting over long horizons, but "long" can mean
years — treat extremes as information about *positioning and regime*, not as a
trade signal on their own.

## Currency debasement is relative, not absolute
All fiat currencies can debase simultaneously. DXY measures the dollar against
other fiat, so it can be flat while every currency loses purchasing power against
real assets. **Gold priced in multiple currencies simultaneously making new highs
is the cleanest evidence of systemic rather than dollar-specific debasement.** If
gold is at a record in USD only, that's a dollar story; at a record in USD, EUR,
JPY, and GBP at once, that's a fiat story.
